Error correction — argumentation language

Each sentence contains one error. Rewrite the corrected version below.

Sentence 1 — discourse marker misuse
I agree with you. Despite, I think we should consider the risks carefully.
Sentence 2 — hedging word order
It could argued be that the contract is ambiguous on this point.
Sentence 3 — concession structure
Although the delay was short, but the financial impact was significant.
Sentence 4 — preposition error
I see where you're coming of, but I'm not fully persuaded.
Sentence 5 — register / collocation
On balance, I make the conclusion that the claimant has a strong case.
Sentence 6 — false concession
Admittedly the evidence is very strong, so the defence will struggle.
Sentence 7 — modal verb
There is a case to make for to settle this dispute out of court.
Sentence 8 — word choice / formality
At the end, I believe the court will rule in our client's favour.
Answer key:
1. "That said" or "However" — Despite needs a noun phrase: "Despite my agreement…"
2. "It could be argued that…" — modal passive word order
3. You can't use although…but together — drop one: "Although the delay was short, the impact was significant."
4. "coming from" — fixed phrase
5. "I conclude that…" — make the conclusion is not a standard collocation
6. Admittedly signals concession before a contrast — the so breaks the logic. Use "but" or "nevertheless."
7. "a case to be made for settling" — gerund after preposition, not infinitive
8. "Ultimately" or "In the end" — At the end means at the end of a specific thing (a book, a film), not finally/ultimately

Debate — the dilemma

Read carefully before you begin. Prepare your argument for Round 1. Round 2 brief will be revealed after.

The dilemma

A lawyer discovers, midway through a case, that their client has been concealing a key piece of information — information that, if disclosed, would significantly weaken their case but might also prevent the court from being misled.

Should the lawyer be legally required to disclose this information to the court, even against the client's wishes?
Round 1 — Your position: YES (argue FOR disclosure)

Argue that the lawyer should be required to disclose. Your key points:

  • The lawyer's duty to the court and to justice overrides their duty to the client in cases of active deception.
  • Professional ethics and bar regulations already mandate candour to the court.
  • Allowing concealment undermines the entire legal system's integrity.

Think about: Are there exceptions? What does Polish law say? What does the client lose vs. what does the system gain?

Round 2 — Now argue the OPPOSITE: NO (argue AGAINST disclosure)

Now defend client confidentiality. Key arguments:

  • Attorney-client privilege is the cornerstone of the legal system.
  • Without it, clients won't be honest with their lawyers — which ultimately harms justice too.
  • The lawyer's role is advocacy, not prosecution.
  • The system has other mechanisms (cross-examination, opposing counsel) to surface the truth.

The challenge: Argue this convincingly even if you personally disagree. That's the real test.

Teacher's brief

Your role as the opposing voice in the debate.

Opposing position — argue AGAINST disclosure (Round 1)

Your key arguments:

  • Attorney-client privilege is the foundation of honest lawyer-client communication.
  • Without it, clients won't tell their lawyers the truth — which ultimately harms justice.
  • The lawyer's job is advocacy, not finding the truth on the court's behalf.
  • Other mechanisms exist: cross-examination, opposing counsel, discovery.

Challenge lines to use:

  • "Where exactly do you draw the line?"
  • "What if disclosing the information ruins an innocent client's case?"
  • "Isn't it the court's job to find the truth — not the lawyer's?"
  • "Can you give me a concrete example of where your rule would apply?"
  • "That's a very idealistic position — how does it work in practice?"

Homework — opinion paragraph

Write 6–8 sentences arguing FOR or AGAINST the statement below. Use at least 4 discourse markers (underline or bold them). Aim for formal but natural English — not a legal brief.

"Lawyers have a greater duty to the justice system than to their individual clients."
It could be argued that a lawyer's primary obligation is to the justice system rather than to any individual client. First and foremost, the legal profession derives its authority and public trust from its commitment to upholding the law — not from winning cases at any cost. Furthermore, rules of professional conduct in most jurisdictions already impose a duty of candour to the court, which implicitly places justice above client loyalty in certain circumstances. That said, one must concede that attorney-client privilege is not merely a technicality; it is the foundation upon which honest communication between lawyer and client depends. Without it, clients would be reluctant to disclose information that their lawyers need to advise them properly. On balance, however, I believe that when client instructions actively conflict with the integrity of the judicial process, the lawyer's duty to the court must prevail. The fact remains that a lawyer who facilitates deception does not merely harm an opposing party — they undermine the system that grants them the right to practise.

Discourse markers used: It could be argued that / First and foremost / Furthermore / That said / On balance / The fact remains that
